What should you know before choosing?

Doors, windows, electrical panels, receptacles, switches, loft ladders and stairways all compete for wall space. Shelving and workbenches should be planned while those elements can still be coordinated.

ASI Storage Is More Than a Factory Shelf Add-On

Low-cost prefab shed packages often use simple workbenches and shelf runs as basic accessories. Appalachian Sheds can offer those straightforward solutions, but premium systems should be defined by depth, materials, framing attachment, finish and intended use.

We do not use vague load claims where the actual assembly has not been specifically designed and documented for that load.

Wall-Mounted Utility Shelving

Utility shelving is the most efficient way to get boxes, small bins, garden supplies, hardware and everyday storage off the floor. Shallow shelves preserve aisle space and make stored items easier to see and reach.

For Appalachian Sheds, wall-mounted shelving should be fastened into structural framing or planned blocking—not merely attached to interior finish material.

12–16 Inches for General Utility Storage

Shallower shelves work well for cans, small boxes, garden products, hardware and items that should remain visible rather than disappearing behind a second row.

Hardware, paint and small storage bins

PART 02

Deep Tote & Bulk Storage Shelving

Tote storage needs more depth than ordinary utility shelving. When large bins, coolers, bulky garden supplies or boxed seasonal items are the priority, deeper shelves can dramatically improve organization—but they also consume more aisle width.

Depth should be selected around the actual containers being used and the clear walking space that remains after installation.

20–24 Inches for Larger Bins & Totes

Twenty-four-inch shelving is a strong planning direction for common large totes when the building width and aisle clearance support it.

Large storage totes and seasonal bins

Workbench Systems

A workbench should be planned as a working surface, not simply a wide shelf. Depth, height, rigidity, electrical access, lighting and what will happen on the bench all affect usability.

A general-purpose standing woodworking bench often falls around the mid-30-inch height range, but the ideal height changes with the user's stature and the task. The final height should be selected intentionally rather than assumed.

24–30 Inches for General Workshop Use

Twenty-four inches preserves more floor space; deeper benches provide more staging area but require greater wall depth and reach.

Choose height around task and primary user

PART 03

Pegboard & Tool-Organization Walls

Pegboard and tool walls put frequently used hand tools where they can be seen and reached quickly. They work especially well above a workbench, beside a garden-tool zone or in a dedicated workshop bay.

The system should be installed over framing or appropriate backing so hooks, rails and accessories are not relying on a thin decorative wall layer alone.

Cabinet & Enclosed Storage

Cabinets keep smaller tools, chemicals, hardware and visually distracting supplies behind doors. They make the most sense in premium workshops, pool houses, offices, studios and finished interiors where appearance matters as much as raw storage capacity.

Cabinet pricing varies dramatically with material, size, finish, drawers, hardware and whether the system is stock, semi-custom or fully built in.

Integrated Custom Storage Layouts

Custom storage combines shelves, tote bays, workbenches, pegboard, cabinets and open floor space into one coordinated interior plan. This is the strongest approach when the building has several jobs to perform at once.

The layout should start with door access, equipment size and movement paths before filling the walls with storage.

Workshop. Garden. Equipment. Hobby. Multi-Use.

Custom layouts are priced after the actual building size, storage goals, electrical plan and interior-use zones are established.

PART 04

Storage Planning Details That Matter Before Construction

The storage system should support the building's real use without creating conflicts elsewhere. These decisions are easiest to make before electrical, windows, loft access and interior finishes are finalized.

Compare the Storage Systems Before Filling the Walls

The best value usually comes from giving each wall a specific job rather than adding storage everywhere.

Plan the Interior Around the Equipment and Storage You Actually Own

Appalachian Sheds can coordinate shelving, tote storage, workbenches, tool walls, cabinets, loft access, doors, windows and electrical locations before the final written scope is approved.

IMPORTANT PROJECT DISTINCTION

How this relates to an Appalachian Sheds project

Built on your property

All Appalachian Sheds buildings are constructed on site. Property access, drainage, topography, location restrictions and the selected model can affect planning.

Exterior shell versus finished interior

Our standard building proposal describes a weather-tight exterior shell. Insulation, electrical, plumbing, heating, cooling, and interior wall or ceiling finishes are separate work unless expressly listed in the written scope.

Only the written scope is authoritative

Model-specific specifications, pricing, selected options, finishing requirements and any engineering or permit obligations must be confirmed for the actual building and site. Avoid treating this general guide as an engineering design or a permit determination.
