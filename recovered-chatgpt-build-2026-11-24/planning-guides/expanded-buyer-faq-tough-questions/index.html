PART 01

The essentials

Direct answers to the practical questions serious homeowners should ask before committing to a backyard building project.

A serious buyer should not be afraid to ask difficult questions, and a serious builder should not be afraid to answer them. Price, deposits, written scope, permits, HOA requirements, site conditions, references, construction standards, warranty limitations and long-term maintenance all deserve clear discussion before work begins.

This guide is designed to help you evaluate those issues without pressure. The goal is not simply to find a company willing to build something in your backyard. The goal is to understand exactly what you are buying, what responsibilities belong to you, what the builder controls, and whether the proposed project truly fits your property, priorities and budget.

PART 02

What Tough Questions Should a Serious Buyer Ask?

The questions that create clarity before construction are usually more important than the questions asked after a problem appears.

Ask who is building the structure, exactly what the price includes, what is excluded, what the deposit reserves, when additional payments are due, how changes are documented, what site preparation is required, whether permits or HOA approvals may apply, what warranty coverage actually says, what maintenance will be required and how the proposed building compares with less expensive alternatives.

A strong answer should make the project easier to understand. It should separate facts from assumptions, define responsibilities and put important terms in writing. If the conversation creates more confusion, pressure or uncertainty, keep asking questions before committing.

PART 03

The Questions That Decide Whether You Can Trust the Proposal

Most buyer concerns eventually come back to three issues: who is responsible, what is included and what happens when conditions change.

PART 04

Six Areas Every Serious Buyer Should Understand

A well-informed buying decision requires more than choosing a building style and accepting a price.

Who is the company? Who will work on your property? What experience supports the project? Are references and insurance information available where appropriate?

What does the price include? What is excluded? What does the deposit accomplish? When are later payments due and what conditions can change the total?

PART 05

Understand What Is Behind the Exterior

Appearance matters, but long-term confidence comes from understanding the entire construction system.

Ask for model-specific written specifications. Do not assume that every building style, size, roof configuration or upgrade uses identical construction details.

PART 06

Warning Signs Serious Buyers Should Not Ignore

These warning signs apply broadly to backyard structures and other residential construction purchases.

No Written Scope

Verbal promises are easy to misunderstand. Important features, exclusions, options, site assumptions and payment terms should be documented.

Pressure to Pay Immediately

A homeowner should have enough time to understand the proposal and payment terms before committing funds.

Full Payment Before Work

Ask how deposits, progress payments and completion payments are structured and what each payment represents.

No Insurance Discussion

A contractor working on private property should be willing to discuss appropriate insurance and project accountability.

IMPORTANT PROJECT DISTINCTION

How this relates to an Appalachian Sheds project

Built on your property

All Appalachian Sheds buildings are constructed on site. Property access, drainage, topography, location restrictions and the selected model can affect planning.

Exterior shell versus finished interior

Our standard building proposal describes a weather-tight exterior shell. Insulation, electrical, plumbing, heating, cooling, and interior wall or ceiling finishes are separate work unless expressly listed in the written scope.

Only the written scope is authoritative

Model-specific specifications, pricing, selected options, finishing requirements and any engineering or permit obligations must be confirmed for the actual building and site. Avoid treating this general guide as an engineering design or a permit determination.
